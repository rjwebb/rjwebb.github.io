What is Canvas?

written 17th November 2026

Currently listening: Mouth Dreams by Neil Cicierega

At my job at canvas.xyz, we spent three years developing a system called canvas. We ended up with something that worked, but its particular combination of strengths and weaknesses didn’t seem to correspond to any real-life use cases that we managed to find. I think the best thing to do now is to describe it, to document it, to turn it into a form where someone can read an article or a wiki about it and maybe a fragment of what we discovered can solve part of someone else’s problem. I’m always surprised when I share some kind of creative work and someone else ends up being surprised or intrigued by a part that I thought was mundane.

Just give me the elevator pitch

The “About” section in the Canvas repo calls it an “Instant database and sync engine for decentralized and local-first applications”. It’s a true, concise but incomplete description. It gives me the urge to expound at length on why it’s not the whole story. Before I start with that, I’d like to produce a few more pithy pitches, from different angles:

  • A framework for building peer-to-peer web apps that makes most of the hard decisions for you
  • A local-first append-only log for distributed communities
  • The thing you actually wanted when you decided to “put it on the blockchain”
  • Networking and database infrastructure for Byzantine fault-tolerant applications
  • One of those Merkle tree things
  • Something between the Blockchain and BitTorrent
  • Firebase but it doesn’t have a centralised server

See what it is yet? No? Let’s go back to the beginning!

The problem

I didn’t found Canvas, but I can attempt to explain what motivated all of this. Canvas originated in late 2021/early 2022. The Biden era. Elon Musk was about to buy Twitter. Bluesky was still in development, not to be released to the public until 2023/2024. Mastodon existed, but it was primarily the stomping ground of people who objected to the fact that Twitter was a private company or wanted to talk about things that would have gotten banned on Twitter. Subcultures, niches, fringe political tendencies. Crypto was at its peak. Sam Bankman-Fried was a free man. There were definitely people rolling their eyes at the decadence of crypto culture (for the sake of my soul I am glad I steered clear of that, although my bank account would beg to differ), but if you said something like “the future of social media will be decentralised” in technical circles, you would probably get sage nods of approval, followed by a question: How do you actually propose to do that?

Let’s start with centralised social media - that’s stuff like Twitter, Facebook, MySpace. You can sign up and log in, make posts, comment on posts, “like”, “react” etc. There is a single authoritative record of what users, posts and reactions “exist”. You probably use an app created by the same company that created the website and all of the code that runs on the server. Parts of this might be implemented in a sort of distributed way, e.g. the database that holds all of the posts might be spread across multiple servers instead of just one big server. There are some great reasons to do this:

  • Reduce read latency: If you have an application where data is read many more times than it is written, you can create “read replica” databases that are synchronised with the “primary database”. They are just used for servicing read requests.
  • Guarantee a certain level of availability: Let’s say your server goes down because of a hardware failure. Your customers can no longer access their data. You can protect against this by mirroring your data, i.e. have multiple databases that are kept in sync (perhaps we have a primary database which the secondary databases copy, or queries are fanned out to all of the databases in tandem). When one database goes down, requests can be served by the others.
  • Exceed limitations that come from using a single server to store all of the data: let’s say the model of server you have available can only serve a certain number of requests per second, or only a certain amount of storage can be attached to it. You can serve larger workloads by splitting the work between multiple servers through “sharding” - this is where each server has the same kinds of tables but we put one fraction of the rows of each table into each of the database “shards”. It helps if you can do this in a way where each queries involves at most one server.

Is this decentralisation? No, not really. The servers are all under control of the same company. They probably live in the same database or region or at the very least use the same cloud provider. The “compute” part of the backend can also be distributed, of course. It kind of doesn’t matter - the question of centralisation or decentralisation concerns where the “truth” lives and who controls it.

Canvas’s solution lies somewhere in the space between Secure Scuttlebutt, CRDTs and IPFS… I’d like to discuss all of these, what they can and can’t do, but this is a HUGE topic!
